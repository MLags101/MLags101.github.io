AE 1601 Glider

About

This glider was the end result of a project for my AE 1601(Intro to Aerospace Engineering) class in my first year. The goal of the project was to learn about the basic principles of flight and how these principles influence design decisions in an actual aircraft. The culmination of the project was a fun competition to see which glider flew the furthest, which my glider won. Additionally, I utilized the Invention Studio to develop my glider, particularly the laser cutters and scrap cardboard and mdf which I used to make prototypes and iterate on my design.

For my AE 1601 (Intro to Aerospace Engineering) course in my first year, I designed and built a glider to explore the fundamental features and principles of gliders and their impact on performance. The project culminated in a competition to determine which glider could fly the furthest, which my design won. Using the Invention Studio's resources, including laser cutters and scrap materials like cardboard and MDF, I iterated on prototypes to create a high-performing glider.
The project took place over the course over several weeks, over which the professor gradually introduced aerospace design concepts that would give us first-years a better idea of what professional engineers do in their work. We went over concepts like the effects of aspect ratio, wing tapering, wing sweeping, etc. We were given basic materials for making the glider consisting of balsa wood. However, we were given a limited amount of balsa wood, so I was nervous about rushing to production, but I knew I still wanted to be able to make prototypes to test my designs. As a result, I turned to the Invention Studio and a lot of thrown away cardboard to make my glider designs.


I tried out many different features to see what worked, learning with every crash. Using the Invention Studio's laser cutters, I prototyped components with scrap cardboard and MDF, allowing rapid iteration. Early prototypes revealed issues with wing stability, as some designs fluttered during test launches. I adjusted the wing dihedral angle and added a stabilizing tail, applying principles of aerodynamic stability from AE 1601. Each prototype was tested by hand-launching, and I recorded flight distances to quantify improvements, ensuring a data-driven approach. Additionally, I integrated some custom designed and 3D printed components to achieve features such as the precise degree dihedral, as well as a hub to add weight to shift the center of mass forward of my center of lift.
At the competition, I got consistent and good results from each of my throws, winning the small competition. It was so much fun to do that process for the first time in my aerospace major, as well as to see the cool other designs of my fellow students. It was a great introduction to the major and set me on my way to continue to grow as an engineer.
