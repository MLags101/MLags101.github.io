## Overview

Kermit V2 is a custom Pixhawk-based quadcopter that uses OpenHD for telemetry and high-quality video. It's the second iteration of my Kermit series, built to practice and improve my technical abilities with UAVs.

[Kermit V1](/projects/quadfrog) was a fully 3D-printed frame with only the basic components of a small UAV, so I could get familiar with how each one works. It also carried a Raspberry Pi underneath, which I used as a Wi-Fi access point for telemetry and some experiments with autonomous control. I learned more drone basics in my first VIP project, on Urban Air Mobility.

Kermit V2 had three objectives:

- **Robust frame design** with materials like carbon fiber.
- **A much better, longer-range comms system**, including a high-quality camera feed.
- **Higher-end propulsion**, so the drone could be larger, carry more payload, and stay stable in wind.

## A modular frame

I wanted to easily upgrade the frame and mount different payloads as I learn new hardware, so modularity was key. The core frame has a range of mounting holes, and the underside has custom 3D-printed **picatinny rails**. I mounted my telemetry antennas there, but they could just as easily hold a separate camera payload or a sensor suite.

## Working with carbon fiber

The carbon fiber came as long bar stock, which I processed at the Invention Studio with water, masking tape and the other protections this material needs. Cutting carbon fiber can throw off extremely thin shards and a dust that's harmful to breathe, so cutting wet contains the contaminants.

To save weight I used a small number of larger screws that run through the entire frame, clamping the arms and battery together. That was strong, but the downside was a full disassembly whenever I needed to change the Raspberry Pi, the power distribution or the GPS.

## OpenHD: long-range digital video

OpenHD made for an interesting experience. It relies on a specific set of Wi-Fi chips in USB adapters and changes how they transmit to mimic an analog link, which greatly increases range and lowers latency. Setting it up took many trial runs, Linux and Raspberry Pi knowledge to troubleshoot, and the ability to diagnose electronic communication problems.

For a long stretch the feed kept cutting out, reviving, then dying again a few seconds later, and I couldn't figure out why. After a series of tests on the power supply, software configuration and wiring, I found the cause. I had made an extended USB cable with a separate power supply so the Wi-Fi radio could transmit at full power, but I hadn't shielded it, so electromagnetic interference was corrupting the packets.

<Callout tone="result" title="Fix">
Wrapping the USB differential pair in aluminium foil and heat shrink shielded it from EMI, and the video stream stabilized.
</Callout>

## Flight test

Development ended with a flight test outside the Invention Studio, where I forgot to bring my GPS, so I couldn't test the autonomous functions. Even so, despite the wind, the drone felt responsive and well balanced on the controls. The landing gear also performed well, giving decent damping at the joints.

## Results

I met the objectives I set out to: I learned to machine carbon fiber and to use OpenHD, achieved high-quality, stable video transmission, and built a drone with a high thrust-to-weight ratio. I'm happy with the final product and already have improvements in mind:

- a frame designed with modification in mind
- easy battery swaps
- a gimballed camera

Many of those became the goals for [Kermit V3](/projects/kermit-v3).
