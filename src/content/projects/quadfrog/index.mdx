## Overview

This project was my hands-on exploration of drones and what they can do. I wanted to grow across as many disciplines as possible by building as much of the drone myself as I could: I designed and fabricated the frame, chose a V-tail configuration, selected and soldered the electronics, built my own ground-station module, and wrote Python and C++ for both the onboard Raspberry Pi and my laptop.

### Motivation

Drones blend mechanical, electrical and software engineering, which is exactly why I love them. The goal was a functional, customizable platform to serve as a foundation for future experiments — the platform that became the Kermit line.

## Frame design

The project began with the V-tail configuration, chosen to improve forward flight and simplify component placement. I designed the frame in Fusion 360, ensuring structural integrity (after a few iterations and lessons learned) and precise motor placement for balanced flight.

## Electronics & software

The onboard flight computer, a Raspberry Pi, ran custom Python to manage flight-control inputs and sensor data, talking to the flight controller over serial. For the ground station I built a box around a Raspberry Pi Pico and an HC-12 radio, paired with Python GCS software I wrote for real-time control and telemetry.

Across iterations I kept improving it: the arms moved from flat plates to an **I-beam section** to account for PLA's weakness, and I added a live camera feed from a webcam on the Pi, streamed over a self-hosted Flask server.

## Fabrication

I printed the frame on the Invention Studio's Ultimakers in PLA, iterating to fix cracking and arm bowing. The final frame was light but robust. I soldered the flight controller, ESCs and motors onto an off-the-shelf power-distribution board.

<Callout tone="lesson">
My first time with high-current LiPo electronics, I blew up an ESC in my dorm and nearly set off the fire alarm. It taught me fast to respect the hardware — and to write procedures before powering up.
</Callout>

## Results

The project merged mechanical design, electronics and programming in one airframe — the V-tail's look was a nod to Watch Dogs 2. I brought Kermit home from college and test-flew it in my backyard. It went well, right up until my inexperience as a pilot put it into a branch. Onward to version 2.
